Why a 3-coloring gives a satisfying assignment

3-SAT as Graph-Coloring — the direction $G_\varphi$ is 3-colorable $\Rightarrow$ $\varphi$ is satisfiable

Notes By Pr. El Hadiq Zouhair

This page shows that whenever the graph $G_\varphi$ admits a proper 3-coloring, the formula $\varphi$ really is satisfiable — and moreover the coloring itself tells us a satisfying assignment.

Fix any proper 3-coloring of $G_\varphi$. Decode it by setting each variable x to True iff its vertex has the same color as the vertex T. Then this assignment makes every clause of $\varphi$ true — so $\varphi$ is satisfiable.

We are given a coloring and must produce a satisfying assignment. The proof is four short steps; the only real work is one small lemma about the OR gadget.

T color = True F color = False B color = Base

Step 1 — The three colors get fixed meanings

The graph contains a base triangle on the vertices T, F, B. Because all three are mutually adjacent, a proper coloring must give them three different colors. We name those colors after the vertices that carry them: the T-color, the F-color, the B-color. From now on "color of a vertex" can be compared to these three anchors.

The base triangle forces three distinct colors — the T, F, B anchors.

Step 2 — Every literal is a consistent True / False

For each variable, $G_\varphi$ has a variable triangle x – ~x – B. Both literal vertices are joined to B, so neither can be the B-color: each is either the T-color or the F-color. And since x and ~x are joined to each other, they have different colors — one is the T-color, the other the F-color.

A variable gadget: x and ~x avoid B and land on opposite truth values.

Consequence: the decoded assignment is well defined and consistent — every variable gets exactly one of True / False, and x, ~x are never both true. So we really do have a genuine truth assignment to test.

Step 3 — The OR-gadget lemma

This is the only computation we need. Recall an OR gadget on inputs u, v: a triangle a – b – out with wires a – u and b – v.

In any proper coloring, if both inputs u and v have the F-color, then out must have the F-color.

Why. The wire a – u forbids a the F-color (since u is F), so a is the T-color or the B-color. Likewise b is the T-color or the B-color. But a and b are adjacent, so they are different — together they use exactly the T-color and the B-color. The triangle's third vertex out must take the one remaining color: the F-color.

Why only this case? The lemma deliberately treats only "both inputs F". For this direction we never ask what out can be when an input is T — Step 4 needs nothing more than "both inputs F ⟹ out is F" (applied twice along the chain). The other three input cases (at least one input T, where out is then free to be T, F, or even B) are exactly what the converse direction uses to build a colouring, and they are handled there. So nothing is missing here: one implication is all this proof requires.

Both inputs F ⟹ a, b use T and B ⟹ out is forced to F.

Step 4 — Every clause contains a true literal

Take any clause $(\ell_1 \lor \ell_2 \lor \ell_3)$. Its gadget builds $o_1 = \mathrm{OR}(\ell_1,\ell_2)$ and $o_2 = \mathrm{OR}(o_1,\ell_3)$, and joins the final output $o_2$ to both F and B.

  • Because $o_2$ is joined to F and B, it can be neither the F-color nor the B-color. So $o_2$ has the T-color.
  • Now suppose, for contradiction, that all three literals $\ell_1, \ell_2, \ell_3$ had the F-color. Then:
    • $o_1 = \mathrm{OR}(\ell_1, \ell_2)$ has both inputs F-colored, so by the Lemma $o_1$ has the F-color.
    • $o_2 = \mathrm{OR}(o_1, \ell_3)$ then has both inputs F-colored ($o_1$ and $\ell_3$), so by the Lemma $o_2$ has the F-color.
    But we just showed $o_2$ has the T-color — a contradiction.
  • Therefore not all three literals are F-colored: at least one literal has the T-color. By our decoding, that literal is True, so the clause is satisfied.

The forced T-color at o₂ cannot survive if all three literals are F — so one literal must be T (true).

Step 4 holds for every clause, so the decoded assignment makes every clause true — i.e. it satisfies $\varphi$. Therefore: if $G_\varphi$ is 3-colorable, then $\varphi$ is satisfiable. $\blacksquare$

A concrete example

Here is $G_\varphi$ for the one-clause formula $\varphi = (x \lor y \lor z)$, in an actual proper 3-coloring. Reading the rule "True iff colored like T" off the picture gives $x = y = z = $ True, and indeed $(x \lor y \lor z)$ is true — the coloring handed us a satisfying assignment, exactly as the proof promises.

Green = T-color. Here x, y, z are all green, so the coloring says $x=y=z=$ True.

The other direction

→ companion page: the converse (satisfiable ⟹ 3-colorable)

We have proved $G_\varphi$ 3-colorable $\Rightarrow \varphi$ satisfiable. The converse ($\varphi$ satisfiable $\Rightarrow G_\varphi$ 3-colorable) is shown on the companion page: starting from a satisfying assignment, color each literal by T or F to match its truth value, color the base triangle, and check that every gadget can then be completed. Together the two directions give the full equivalence $$G_\varphi \text{ is 3-colorable} \iff \varphi \text{ is satisfiable},$$ which is exactly why solving graph 3-coloring would solve 3-SAT.
