0. The whole course in two questions

Before any counting problem, ask exactly two questions:

  1. Does the ORDER of the chosen items matter?
  2. Can the same item be chosen MORE THAN ONCE (repetition)?
Order?Repetition?FormulaName
YesYes$n^k$Strings / codes
YesNo$P(n,k) = \dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}$$k$-permutation
NoNo$\dbinom{n}{k} = \dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$$k$-combination
NoYes$\dbinom{n+k-1}{k}$Stars & bars
Order matters → there is a position, a rank, a word, a podium. Order ignored → it is a team, a subset, a handful. Repetition → "with replacement", "as many times as you like".

1. Sum rule — "OR" means add

If a choice is made from case A or case B (cases that cannot overlap), the counts add: $\;N = N_A + N_B$.
the problem splits into disjoint scenarios — you do one of them, never both. Keyword: "or", "either … or", counting by cases.
1. Choose a math course ($12$ options) or a physics course ($9$ options): $12 + 9 = 21$ choices.
2. Travel by train ($5$ departures) or by bus ($7$ departures): $5 + 7 = 12$ ways.
3. Pick one book from $4$ novels or $6$ biographies: $4 + 6 = 10$.
If the cases can overlap, do NOT just add — subtract the overlap: $|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|$.
3 / 23

2. Product rule — "THEN / AND" means multiply

If a task is a sequence of steps — $m$ ways for step 1, then $n$ ways for step 2 (whatever step 1 was) — the counts multiply: $\;N = m \cdot n$.
you build the outcome step by step and every step is free of the previous ones. Keywords: "then", "and", "for each", successive choices.
1. Outfit = shirt ($4$) then pants ($3$): $4 \times 3 = 12$ outfits.
2. Full menu = starter ($3$) and main ($5$) and dessert ($2$): $3 \times 5 \times 2 = 30$ meals.
3. $4$-letter lowercase password: $26 \times 26 \times 26 \times 26 = 26^4 = 456{,}976$.
OR → $+$   |   AND/THEN → $\times$. Most problems are a mix: multiply inside each case, add across the cases.

3. Ordered + repetition allowed → $n^k$

Filling $k$ positions, each with any of $n$ symbols, repetition allowed: $\;n^k$ outcomes.
you form codes, PINs, words, bit strings — each slot is independent and symbols may repeat.
1. $4$-digit PIN codes: $10^4 = 10{,}000$.
2. Binary strings of length $8$: $2^8 = 256$.
3. License plates of $3$ letters: $26^3 = 17{,}576$.
"$n$ choices, $k$ times" → $n^k$. It is just the product rule applied $k$ times.

4. Arrange ALL $n$ distinct objects → $n!$

The number of ways to put $n$ distinct objects in a row is $\;n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 1$, with $0! = 1$.
everything gets a position: rankings, queues, shelf arrangements, seatings in a row.
1. $9$ athletes finish a race: $9! = 362{,}880$ possible rankings.
2. $5$ books on a shelf: $5! = 120$ arrangements.
3. $4$ people in a queue: $4! = 24$ orders.
First position: $n$ choices, next: $n-1$, … last: $1$. Multiply — that's all $n!$ is.
6 / 23

5. Arrange only $k$ of the $n$ → $P(n,k)$

Ordered selection of $k$ distinct items out of $n$: $$P(n,k) = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.$$
only $k$ "seats" exist and the seats are different: podiums, ranked lists, distinct offices/roles.
1. Gold–silver–bronze among $9$ athletes: $P(9,3) = 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 = 504$.
2. President, secretary, treasurer from $10$ people: $P(10,3) = 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 = 720$.
3. $4$-letter words from $7$ distinct letters, no repeats: $P(7,4) = 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 = 840$.
Start at $n$ and multiply $k$ decreasing factors. $P(n,n) = n!$ — arranging everybody is the special case.
7 / 23

6. Arrange with REPEATED items → divide by repeats

$n$ items where identical copies appear in groups of sizes $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k$: $$\frac{n!}{n_1!\, n_2! \cdots n_k!} \quad \text{distinct arrangements.}$$
arranging letters of a word with repeated letters, or any lineup containing indistinguishable copies.
1. "BANANA" ($3$ A, $2$ N, $1$ B): $\dfrac{6!}{3!\,2!} = \dfrac{720}{12} = 60$.
2. "MISSISSIPPI" ($1$ M, $4$ I, $4$ S, $2$ P): $\dfrac{11!}{4!\,4!\,2!} = 34{,}650$.
3. "BOOKKEEPER" ($2$ O, $2$ K, $3$ E): $\dfrac{10!}{2!\,2!\,3!} = 151{,}200$.
Pretend everything is distinct ($n!$), then divide by $n_i!$ for each group of clones — they were overcounted.
8 / 23

7. Arrange around a CIRCLE → $(n-1)!$

$n$ distinct objects around a circle (rotations look the same): $\;(n-1)!$ arrangements. If reflections also look the same (e.g. a necklace): $\;(n-1)!/2$ for $n \geq 3$.
round tables, necklaces, keyrings — anything where "rotating everyone one seat" changes nothing.
1. $6$ guests at a round table: $(6-1)! = 5! = 120$.
2. $8$ keys on a keyring (rotations and flips equal): $\dfrac{7!}{2} = 2{,}520$.
Fix one person as an anchor — only the other $n-1$ really choose a seat.
9 / 23

8. Choose a SUBSET (order ignored) → $\binom{n}{k}$

Unordered selection of $k$ items out of $n$: $$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} = \frac{P(n,k)}{k!}.$$
you pick a group and nobody inside it has a special role: teams, hands of cards, lottery tickets, pizza toppings.
1. Team of $5$ from $12$ players: $\binom{12}{5} = 792$.
2. $3$ pizza toppings out of $8$: $\binom{8}{3} = 56$.
3. Lottery: $6$ numbers out of $49$: $\binom{49}{6} = 13{,}983{,}816$.
Combination = permutation with the order "erased": divide $P(n,k)$ by the $k!$ orderings of the chosen group.

9. Choose with REPETITION (order ignored) → stars & bars

Number of ways to pick $k$ items from $n$ types, repetition allowed — equivalently, solutions of $x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n = k$ in non-negative integers: $$\binom{n+k-1}{k}.$$
distributing identical objects into distinct boxes, or shopping where flavours can repeat. Keywords: "identical", "any number of each".
1. $10$ identical candies for $4$ children: $\binom{4+10-1}{10} = \binom{13}{10} = 286$.
2. A dozen donuts from $5$ flavours: $\binom{5+12-1}{12} = \binom{16}{12} = 1{,}820$.
3. Solutions of $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 7$, $x_i \geq 0$: $\binom{3+7-1}{7} = \binom{9}{7} = \binom{9}{2} = 36$.
Draw $k$ stars (the items) and $n-1$ bars (the walls between types): $\star\star|\,|\star\star \;\to\; (2,0,2)$. Choose where the stars go among $n+k-1$ slots.

10. "Exactly … of each kind" → choose per group, multiply

To build a selection with quotas from several categories: choose inside each category separately with $\binom{\cdot}{\cdot}$, then multiply the counts (product rule).
the problem says "exactly $a$ of type X and $b$ of type Y" — committees with quotas, card hands with fixed ranks.
1. Team of $5$: exactly $2$ Swiss (of $4$), $2$ Ethiopians (of $5$), $1$ Brazilian (of $3$): $\binom{4}{2}\binom{5}{2}\binom{3}{1} = 6 \cdot 10 \cdot 3 = 180$.
2. Committee of $3$ men (of $6$) and $2$ women (of $7$): $\binom{6}{3}\binom{7}{2} = 20 \cdot 21 = 420$.
3. $5$-card hands with exactly $2$ aces: $\binom{4}{2}\binom{48}{3} = 6 \cdot 17{,}296 = 103{,}776$.
"Exactly" → split into groups, choose, multiply. "At least / at most" → add the "exactly" cases, or count the complement.

11. Binomial theorem — read coefficients directly

$$(x+y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^{k}.$$ The coefficient of $x^{n-k}y^k$ is $\binom{n}{k}$ — no expansion needed.
asked for one coefficient of a power of a binomial. If the binomial has constants inside, keep their powers!
1. Coefficient of $x^3y^4$ in $(x+y)^7$: $\binom{7}{4} = 35$.
2. Coefficient of $x^5$ in $(2+x)^9$: $\binom{9}{5}\,2^{9-5} = 126 \cdot 16 = 2{,}016$.
3. Coefficient of $x^2$ in $(3x-2)^5$: $\binom{5}{2}(3)^2(-2)^3 = 10 \cdot 9 \cdot (-8) = -720$.
Template: term $= \binom{n}{k} (\text{first})^{n-k} (\text{second})^{k}$. Match the power of $x$ you need, then plug in.

12. Symmetry — $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$

$$\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}.$$ Choosing $k$ to keep = choosing $n-k$ to throw away.
$k$ is close to $n$ — flip it to a small number and compute in one line.
1. $\binom{100}{98} = \binom{100}{2} = \dfrac{100 \cdot 99}{2} = 4{,}950$.
2. $\binom{20}{13} = \binom{20}{7} = 77{,}520$.
3. $\binom{50}{49} = \binom{50}{1} = 50$.
Always shrink $k$: never compute $\binom{n}{k}$ with $k > n/2$ by hand.

13. Pascal — split on one special element

$$\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}.$$ (selections containing a fixed item) $+$ (selections without it).
one element is special ("my favourite stock", "the captain") — split total count into with it / without it. Also: build Pascal's triangle row by row.
1. Portfolios of $8$ stocks out of $20$: total $\binom{20}{8} = 125{,}970$; containing the favourite: $\binom{19}{7} = 50{,}388$; avoiding it: $\binom{19}{8} = 75{,}582$. Check: $50{,}388 + 75{,}582 = 125{,}970$. ✓
2. Row $5$ of Pascal's triangle from row $4$ ($1,4,6,4,1$): $1,\;1{+}4,\;4{+}6,\;6{+}4,\;4{+}1,\;1 = 1,5,10,10,5,1$.
3. Teams of $5$ from $12$ that include the captain: $\binom{11}{4} = 330$; that exclude her: $\binom{11}{5} = 462$; total $792 = \binom{12}{5}$. ✓
"With or without the special one" — the most useful splitting trick in counting.

14. Absorption — $k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$

$$k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}.$$
a stray factor $k$ multiplies a binomial (typical in sums), or you want to compute $\binom{n}{k}$ recursively. Story: "committee of $k$ with one chair" counted two ways.
1. Committees of $4$ from $10$ people, with a chair: $4\binom{10}{4} = 4 \cdot 210 = 840$, or chair first: $10\binom{9}{3} = 10 \cdot 84 = 840$. Same. ✓
2. Compute along a row: $\binom{10}{3} = \frac{10}{3}\binom{9}{2} = \frac{10}{3}\cdot 36 = 120$.
3. Simplify $\sum_k k\binom{n}{k}$: absorb $k$ to get $n\sum_k \binom{n-1}{k-1} = n\,2^{n-1}$ — one line (see slide 18).
The $k$ gets "absorbed" into the binomial, leaving a clean $n$ outside.

15. The plug-in trick — substitute values in $(x+y)^n$

Choose $x, y$ in the binomial theorem to evaluate sums: $$x{=}y{=}1:\;\sum_k \binom{n}{k} = 2^n \qquad x{=}-1,y{=}1:\;\sum_k (-1)^k\binom{n}{k} = 0 \qquad x{=}2,y{=}1:\;\sum_k 2^k\binom{n}{k} = 3^n$$
a sum runs over all $k$ with powers of a constant times $\binom{n}{k}$ — recognise $(x+y)^n$ in disguise. In general $\sum_k a^k \binom{n}{k} = (1+a)^n$.
1. Number of subsets of $8$ pizza toppings (any size): $2^8 = 256$.
2. Even-size subsets = odd-size subsets $= 2^{n-1}$. For $n=4$: $\binom{4}{0}+\binom{4}{2}+\binom{4}{4} = 1+6+1 = 8 = 2^3$. ✓
3. $\sum_{k=0}^{5} 2^k \binom{5}{k} = 3^5 = 243$.
See $\sum (\text{const})^k \binom{n}{k}$? Don't compute — plug into $(1+a)^n$.

16. The $k$-weighted sums

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n} k\binom{n}{k} = n\,2^{n-1} \qquad\qquad \sum_{k=0}^{n} k^2\binom{n}{k} = n(n+1)\,2^{n-2}.$$
the summand has an extra $k$ or $k^2$. Two routes: absorb the $k$ (slide 14), or differentiate $(1+x)^n = \sum \binom{n}{k}x^k$ and set $x = 1$.
1. $\sum_k k\binom{5}{k} = 5 \cdot 2^4 = 80$. Check by hand: $5 + 20 + 30 + 20 + 5 = 80$. ✓
2. Average size of a random subset of $\{1,\dots,n\}$: $\dfrac{n\,2^{n-1}}{2^n} = \dfrac{n}{2}$.
3. $\sum_k k^2\binom{3}{k} = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 2^1 = 24$. Check: $1{\cdot}3 + 4{\cdot}3 + 9{\cdot}1 = 24$. ✓
Factor $k$ → answer keeps a factor $n$ and loses one power of $2$. ($k^2$ → loses two.)

17. Vandermonde — two groups, one selection

$$\binom{m+n}{r} = \sum_{k=0}^{r} \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{r-k} \qquad\text{special case } m{=}n{=}r:\quad \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}.$$
a sum of products of two binomials whose lower indices add to a constant — it collapses to a single binomial. Story: choose $r$ from a pool split in two parts.
1. Florist with $15$ roses and $15$ lavenders, bouquets of $10$: $\sum_{k}\binom{15}{k}\binom{15}{10-k} = \binom{30}{10} = 30{,}045{,}015$ — and the "exactly 3 roses" term alone is $\binom{15}{3}\binom{15}{7} = 2{,}927{,}925$.
2. Committee of $5$ from $6$ men + $7$ women, all gender splits combined: $\binom{13}{5} = 1{,}287$.
3. $\sum_{k=0}^{5}\binom{5}{k}^2 = 1 + 25 + 100 + 100 + 25 + 1 = 252 = \binom{10}{5}$. ✓
Product of binomials, lower indices summing to $r$ → merge the two upper indices: $\binom{m+n}{r}$.

18. Hockey-stick — sum down a diagonal

$$\sum_{i=r}^{n} \binom{i}{r} = \binom{n+1}{r+1}.$$
you sum binomials with constant lower index and growing upper index — a diagonal of Pascal's triangle. The answer sits one step below-and-right: the blade of the stick.
1. $\binom{2}{2}+\binom{3}{2}+\binom{4}{2}+\binom{5}{2}+\binom{6}{2} = 1+3+6+10+15 = 35 = \binom{7}{3}$. ✓
2. Sum of the first triangular numbers $1+3+6+10 = 20 = \binom{6}{3}$.
3. $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + n = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{i}{1} = \binom{n+1}{2}$ — Gauss's formula for free.
Same bottom, growing top → result has both indices bumped by one: $\binom{n+1}{r+1}$.

Binomial identities

Symmetry$\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$ — shrink large $k$.
Pascal$\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$ — with / without a special element.
Absorption$k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ — kill a stray factor $k$.
Row sum$\sum_k \binom{n}{k} = 2^n$ — all subsets.
Alternating$\sum_k (-1)^k\binom{n}{k} = 0$ — even = odd $= 2^{n-1}$.
Plug-in$\sum_k a^k\binom{n}{k} = (1+a)^n$ — e.g. $a=2$ gives $3^n$.
$k$-weighted$\sum_k k\binom{n}{k} = n\,2^{n-1}$; $\;\sum_k k^2\binom{n}{k} = n(n+1)2^{n-2}$.
Vandermonde$\sum_k \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{r-k} = \binom{m+n}{r}$ — two pools merge.
Squares$\sum_k \binom{n}{k}^2 = \binom{2n}{n}$ — Vandermonde with $m{=}n{=}r$.
Hockey-stick$\sum_{i=r}^{n}\binom{i}{r} = \binom{n+1}{r+1}$ — diagonal sum.

20. Spot the tool — self-test (click to reveal)

1. How many ways to seat $5$ friends around a campfire?

Circular → $(5-1)! = 4! = 24$.

2. How many $6$-character passwords from $36$ symbols (repeats allowed)?

Ordered + repetition → $36^6 = 2{,}176{,}782{,}336$.

3. How many anagrams of "COFFEE"?

Repeated letters ($2$ F, $2$ E) → $\dfrac{6!}{2!\,2!} = 180$.

4. How many ways to give $8$ identical pencils to $3$ kids?

Stars & bars → $\binom{3+8-1}{8} = \binom{10}{8} = \binom{10}{2} = 45$.

5. Compute $\sum_{k=0}^{10} 3^k \binom{10}{k}$ without summing.

Plug-in with $a = 3$ → $(1+3)^{10} = 4^{10} = 1{,}048{,}576$.

6. Hands of $4$ cards with exactly $1$ king ($52$-card deck)?

Quotas → $\binom{4}{1}\binom{48}{3} = 4 \cdot 17{,}296 = 69{,}184$.

7. Simplify $\binom{2}{2}+\binom{3}{2}+\cdots+\binom{9}{2}$.

Hockey-stick → $\binom{10}{3} = 120$.
